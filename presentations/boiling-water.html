Boiling Water: Pressure, Altitude & the Open vs. Closed Container

Why a pot of water boils at 100 °C in Los Angeles, at 92 °C in Denver, and way above 100 °C inside a pressure cooker — explained without chemistry jargon.

Use ← → arrow keys, the buttons below, or swipe.
01States of matter

Same molecules, different energy

Water molecules are always moving. Temperature is just a measure of their average speed. How much they move decides whether water is ice, liquid, or steam.

Solid · ice

Locked in a lattice. They only vibrate in place.

Liquid · water

Close together but sliding past each other.

Gas · steam

Free and fast, flying across all the space they get.

02The boiling point

Boiling is an escape

Even cool water evaporates: a few fast molecules slip off the surface. Boiling is different. It's the temperature where molecules throughout the liquid have enough energy to form bubbles of vapor that push back against the air pressing down on the water.

At sea level, that tipping point is 100 °C / 212 °F. Change the pressure and you change the boiling point.

Vapor pressure of the bubbles = pressure of the air → boil

04Latent heat

Where does the heat go?

On the flat parts of the curve, energy isn't raising the temperature. It's breaking the bonds holding molecules together. That hidden energy is called latent heat, and for boiling it's enormous.

Warm 1 kg water by 1 °C
4.19 kJ
Warm 1 kg water 0 → 100 °C
419 kJ
Boil 1 kg water into steam
2,260 kJ

Turning boiling water into steam takes about 5.4× the energy it took to heat it from freezing to boiling. That's why a pot takes so long to boil dry, and why steam burns are so severe.

05Open vs. closed container

Trap the steam, raise the boiling point

Open pot

Steam escapes, so the pressure stays at the atmosphere's 14.7 psi. Water boils at 100 °C and can't get any hotter, however high the flame.

Sealed pot · pressure cooker
Pressure14.7 psi
Boils at100 °C

With the lid sealed, steam builds pressure: ~29.7 psi absolute (15 psi on the gauge) pushes the boiling point to about 121 °C. Hotter water cooks food roughly 2–3× faster.

Danger: if a sealed, superheated pot opens suddenly, the pressure drops and the water flashes to steam all at once — violently. Always vent pressure cookers as instructed.

06Altitude & pressure

Climb higher, boil cooler

The higher you go, the less air is stacked above you, so it presses down less. Bubbles form more easily, and water boils at a lower temperature.

07Why it matters

Real life at lower boiling points

Cooking at altitude

In Denver, boiling water is only ~92 °C, so pasta and eggs take noticeably longer. Recipes add time, liquid, or a pressure cooker.

Airplanes

Cabins are pressurized to the equivalent of ~6,000–8,000 ft, so the in-flight kettle boils around 93 °C. Coffee tastes flatter, partly for this reason.

Tea on Everest

Near base camp, water boils at ~82 °C, too cool to properly steep black tea, which wants water near 100 °C.

Vacuum chambers

Pump out enough air and water boils at room temperature, or even while it's cold enough to start freezing.

08Takeaway

Boiling isn't about heat alone — it's about the balance between molecular energy and the pressure pushing back.

  • Fun fact: on Mars, where air pressure is under 1% of Earth's, liquid water would boil away at just a few degrees above freezing.
  • Fun fact: a microwaved cup of water can superheat past 100 °C without bubbling, then erupt when disturbed.
